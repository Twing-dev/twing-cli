02 The three rules that make it work

1
Nothing reaches an LLM until stage 1 has narrowed it

The free pass runs first and does double duty — it finds real problems on its own, and it tells the LLMs where to look. Roughly ten of the seventeen checks never need a model at all.

2
No LLM ever sees the whole change

Each specialist gets one slice and one question. They run independently, with no shared context — so they can’t agree with each other simply because they saw each other’s answers.

3
Nothing reaches you unverified

Every claim gets challenged by a separate call that sees only that claim and the code it names. Unconfirmed findings are deleted. This is the difference between a tool people trust and a bot people mute.

03 What each LLM is allowed to see

The change as a whole is big and noisy. Each specialist gets a deliberately small window onto it — enough to answer its one question, and nothing more.

04 Why not just one LLM reading the diff?

That is what everyone else does, and it is why people stop reading their output. One prompt over a large change produces a long list of confident-sounding findings — some real, most not.

Once a reviewer hits two or three false ones, they start skimming the rest. The tool is dead from that moment, even though it is still running and still producing output.

Splitting the work into narrow specialists and then challenging everything they say is the entire difference between something a team keeps switched on and something they quietly mute.

05 Cost and speed
